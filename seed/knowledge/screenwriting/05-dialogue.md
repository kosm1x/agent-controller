# Dialogue: action, subtext and the four flaw classes

**Read this when:** writing or polishing dialogue, fixing on-the-nose or expository lines, giving each character a distinct voice, converting a two-hander into a trialogue, writing comedy exchanges, or cutting speeches.
**Source:** distilled 2026-09-12 from jtydhr88/screenwriting-skills `sw-dialogue` (method only); underlying books: Robert McKee's *Dialogue*; Richard Walter; Neill Hicks; Lu Jun (Chinese drama language theory); Lajos Egri; Blake Snyder; screwball-comedy conventions.
**Pairs with:** knowledge/screenwriting/04-character-and-conflict.md, knowledge/screenwriting/06-scene-craft.md, knowledge/screenwriting/13-short-form-commercial.md, knowledge/screenwriting/12-terms.md.

Thesis: **dialogue is action** (McKee). Under every line sit desire, intention and strategy. A dialogue problem is usually a story problem: re-check event and character design (04, 06) before rewriting lines.

## 1. What dialogue is

- **Full definition**: anything a character says to anyone: another character, themselves (inner monologue counts) or the audience. Nobody speaks without reason: desire -> intention -> action = verbal strategy.
- **Dramatized dialogue** (in-scene, action provoking reaction) vs **narratized dialogue** (fourth wall gone).
- **Three functions**: exposition, characterization, action.
- **Three layers**: the **said** (vocabulary, syntax, tone); the **unsaid** (consciously withheld; make it perceptible); the **unsayable** (subconscious need, exposed only by choice under pressure). As language, dialogue delivers characterization; as action, true character.
- **Action vs behaviour**: eating ice cream is behaviour; drowning a sorrow or rewarding oneself is the action. A line is unfinished until its subtextual action is named. Silence is an action; a pause is a reaction.
- **Three questions** per line: What does the character want now? What action gets it? What words carry out that action? Tag the action with a **gerund phrase**.
- **Medium**: theatre is aural (poetic speech accepted); film is visual and the camera magnifies falseness, so lines must be natural, actable, written for actors and open to improvisation (exceptions: stylized realism, non-realism, extreme characters). **Conflict level sets volume**: more external/social conflict = less talk; more private/inner conflict = more.

## 2. Exposition: show it, weaponize it, keep the secret

- Release the minimum a moment needs, never early. Rank every fact; repeat the vital ones.
- **Show vs tell**: a recital of shared history has no purpose; an insult referencing it plants the facts unnoticed.
- **Narrative drive**: leak information drop by drop so the audience guesses forward. **Exposition as ammunition**: a character uses what they know as a weapon when it hurts most.
- **The strongest revelation is a secret**. Backstory is one buried event, saved for the lesser-of-two-evils moment; turning a scene on backstory is the most powerful expository technique.
- **Forced-exposition prohibitions**: crawl titles, newspaper headlines, characters telling each other what both know. Walter's Principle 28: never let a character tell another what the audience already knows.
- Hicks: put explanatory dialogue in an unexpected setting for energy. **The two legal on-the-nose lines**: "What do you want?" and "Are you all right?".
- McKee: what common sense infers stays unwritten; give nothing unless its absence confuses; the secret comes last.

## 3. McKee's six tasks and the four flaw classes

**Six tasks**: each spoken unit performs an inner action; each action/reaction beat builds toward the turning point; statement and implication carry information and viewpoint; a unique verbal style individualizes; progressing beats hold attention; language strikes the reader while staying true to the character.

### 3.1 Credibility flaws
- Conversation wastes words; dialogue concentrates meaning. Credible is not realistic.
- Empty talk (telling what the listener knows); overwrought emotion; over-explicit (writer's knowledge in the character's self-awareness); over-perceptive; **excuses mistaken for motives**: motive is subconscious need, the line is the excuse.
- Melodrama's problem is missing motivation, not excess expression. Once motive matches action, choose direct or oblique.

### 3.2 Language flaws
- **Cliche**: write the first word, improvise wilder ones, keep the best.
- **Character-neutral language**: a generic oath becomes a regional one.
- **Ornate language** advertises the writer and shuts the character; in doubt, lower the register.
- **Dry language, eight cures**: concrete over abstract; familiar over strange; short words over long (more agitated = shorter, more active = shorter, cleverer = more complex, more learned = longer); direct over roundabout; active over passive; short sentences over long; expressive over imitative; cut filler (hey, oh, well, also).
- If a line implies no unspoken thought or feeling, enrich it or cut it.

### 3.3 Content flaws
- **On-the-nose**: subtext written as text; a literal candlelit "I love you" is unplayable. Ever-present subtext is realism's rule; non-realist genres may thin it.
- **Inner-monologue fallacy**: a long uninterrupted speech aimed at no one becomes the author's mouthpiece. Recast as action/reaction with inserted non-verbal reactions; a talker may exhaust the characters, never the audience.
- **Duologue** stiffens like a brick; **convert to trialogue**: give the pair a third thing to fight through.

### 3.4 Design flaws
- **Repetition**: accidental echoes (read aloud); **repeated beats** (same strategy in new words). The cardinal sin is boredom; the third crying scene turns comic.
- **Crippled dialogue**: blurred meaning; mistimed meaning; **mistimed cue**: the last word of a speech should be the **core word** that triggers the reply. Check every final phrase.
- **Mis-designed scenes**: turning point too early, late or absent; **split scenes** (text and subtext diverge: inner life all on the surface, too weak for the words, unrelated, or no conflict). **Rewrite trap**: each pass makes lines more explicit until subtext vanishes; the fix is event and character design, not words (06).

## 4. Technique

- **Figures of speech**: the more sensory the image, the more it sticks.
- **Sentence design by core-word position**: **suspense sentence** (periodic; key idea held to the last word, as a joke lands); **cumulative sentence** (core first; conversational ease); **balanced sentence** (core mid-sentence); parallel design. Any pattern overused becomes wallpaper: **mix**.
- **Economy**: every word works. Walter's Principle 26: a screenwriter is not paid by the word. Per line: advances plot? says something new? leaner way?
- **Pauses**: tighten before the turning point, breathe after; a pause must be earned. Walter: no underlining; ellipses at most twice (only one speaker finishing another's line); no exclamation marks; parentheticals almost never (exception: naming the addressee in a crowd).
- **Silence**: when a scene over-talks, write for the eye: paralanguage (nod, glance, wave) and physical action; a deep breath can be the turning point. Silence is the leanest form of speech.
- **Argument** (Walter's Principle 27): agreement matters in life, not art. Each line challenges the next; never let two people agree; the script is one continuous quarrel.
- **Rhythm**: treat lines as music that happens to carry words; rise, fall, question and pause carry meaning before any word does. Whoever controls the tempo wins.

### 4.1 Walter's prohibition list
- Unforgivable fillers: "I think", "um", "you know", "listen".
- No phonetic dialect spelling.
- Do not chase "natural" ums and ahs.
- **Small talk only when it transcends itself and touches the theme**.
- Long speeches only if every sentence gives information.
- Replace greeting exchanges with lines that do work.

## 5. Character-specific dialogue

- Complex psychology = individual voice. The base unit is **vocabulary**: nothing betrays a character like word choice and the names for the things in their life. Work inside-out: content -> form -> effect.
- **Grammar**: **nouns and verbs expose range of knowledge** (one word for a nail vs six); **modifiers, voice and modality expose personality** (huge vs magnificent; active vs passive; "could" vs "must").
- **Culture**: give the character a cultural token as their third thing.
- **Creative-limitation principle**: the first stale sentence is easier than the image beneath it; even a dull character needs a vividly written dullness.
- **Snyder's cover-the-names test**: hide the names; can you tell who speaks? All sound like the writer = fail. One linguistic trait per character.
- Egri: dialogue grows from three-dimensional character and conflict; economical; **sacrifice the great line for the character**; fit the profession; no lectures; jokes serve another function.
- Hoxter: speech exposes class, education, occupation, gender, ethnicity; a group is not uniform. Lu Jun: hear the voice, know the person.

## 6. Lu Jun: language must be beautiful

- Spoken drama nears life speech, opera balances song and speech; the shared rule is beauty. **Elegant beauty = poeticized** (imagistic, distilled).
- **Plain (native) beauty may matter more**: an audience cannot pause. Plain is not crude: shallow words, deep meaning. The test is fit to character.
- **Three requirements**: (1) **action** (a line so charged nobody could interject); (2) **characterization** (worst fault: a line anyone could say); (3) **subtext** (Liu Zhiji: near words, far meaning). Lao She: how outranks what; one sigh beats a torrent.

## 7. Comedy dialogue

- Comic characters run on **blind obsession**; rigid thinking is funny. Comedy needs no empathy: keep the audience cool and at a safe distance. Comic leads lack subconscious self-contradiction; appearance vs reality replaces it. Minor characters get one trait.
- **Four techniques**: clarity (fewest, clearest words); exaggeration (small made huge or huge small); timing (setup/payoff; the punch ends the line); incongruity (adults fighting like children). Once subtext has risen to text, on-the-nose is allowed; spiral down personality -> appearance -> emotion -> violence. Three comic energies: defensive feeling, aggressive feeling, sex.
- **Screwball**: leads sparring wit for wit; the heroine never loses the exchange; sexual subtext under the banter; breakneck delivery; lines double as setups.
- Indick: character psychology beats taboo for laughs. Field: comedy = a situation taken with deadly seriousness.

## 8. Dialogue in the scene: McKee's beat-analysis method

1. Per character: **desire / antagonism / scene intention / strategy** (conscious) + **super-intention** (subconscious, often the reverse of the characterization).
2. List the 2-3 values at stake, mark the opening charge; write each beat as action/reaction gerunds + subtext + value change; no repeated beat, every beat escalates, turning point on time.
3. Contrast the two characters by content, length, rhythm.
4. Six conflict shapes: **balanced**, **comic**, **asymmetric** (one presses, one evades), **indirect** (hostility steered through polite talk), **reflexive** (speaking self vs silent self), **minimal** (confession replaces argument).
5. Story first: inciting incident -> core value -> five dimensions of desire -> four levels of antagonism -> spine -> a turning point per scene -> beats -> **five steps of behaviour** (desire -> awareness of antagonism -> choice -> action -> expression).
6. High- vs low-context cultures set the text/subtext ratio.

## 9. Dialogue diagnostic
(13 questions, as in the source)

1. What action sits under this line (gerund)? What does the character want, by what strategy? No answer = cut or rewrite.
2. Cover the names: can you tell who speaks? Do nouns/verbs show knowledge, modifiers personality?
3. Is anyone telling another what both know? Is it ammunition? If not, cut.
4. Anything on-the-nose? Exempt only "What do you want?", "Are you all right?" and non-realist genres.
5. Is the two-hander stiff? Find a third thing; make it a trialogue.
6. How often does the same beat repeat?
7. Is the last word of each speech the core word? Is meaning timed right?
8. Any "I think", "you know", "listen", "hey", "oh", phonetic dialect, underlining, exclamation marks, a third ellipsis, parenthetical acting notes?
9. Does every sentence of a long speech give new information? Can it become action/reaction beats?
10. Do two people agree anywhere? Agreement is boredom.
11. Could this scene work without dialogue: a breath, a ring removed?
12. Cliche: was this your first choice?
13. Is the melodramatic feel weak motivation? Is the oblique version crueller?

## 10. Training

- Eavesdrop for text and subtext; collect and coin words; read good scripts; **rewrite bad dialogue** (the fastest training).
- **Write from inside the character**: Stanislavski's question is "if I were this character in this situation, what would I do?"; write inside-out, read outside-in, then once as a first-time audience.
- **Hicks's five-step exercise**: two pages of dialogue ending in an "...okay?" question -> rewrite with **no pronouns** except first and last lines -> rewrite **removing every negative statement** -> transcribe ten minutes of real talk, compress ten pages to two -> hand-copy two pages of a master's dialogue for rhythm.
- **Hoxter exercises**: stalling (three strategies vs three counters); the power of silence; two wants, three beats, one polarity reversal, a winner, dialogue only.
- McKee's key-question list (desire, super-intention, motivation, antagonism, values, subtext, beats, turning point): ask from each character's viewpoint, before, during and after writing.
