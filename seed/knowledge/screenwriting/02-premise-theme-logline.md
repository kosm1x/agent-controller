# Premise, theme, controlling idea and logline
**Read this when:** turning an idea into a premise, finding what a story is about, choosing subject and angle, testing a logline, deciding the ending from the theme, or diagnosing "events but no story".
**Source:** distilled 2026-09-12 from jtydhr88/screenwriting-skills `sw-premise-theme` (method only); underlying books: Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing; McKee, Story; Hicks, Screenwriting 101; Cron, Story Genius; Hoxter's twelve-rule screenwriting manual; Snyder, Save the Cat!; Lu Jun, Playwriting Theory and Technique ch. 1-6.
**Pairs with:** knowledge/screenwriting/01-workflow-and-story-bible.md, knowledge/screenwriting/03-story-structure.md, knowledge/screenwriting/09-series-engine-and-bible.md, knowledge/screenwriting/12-terms.md.

## 1. The premise is a tyrant (Egri)
1. Every second of a play has a premise; action without a known motive is frenzy.
2. Three parts: character trait + "leads to" + outcome. Thrift leads to waste: trait = character, "leads to" = conflict, waste = ending.
3. Lists lack your conviction: which side are you on? Never dramatise what you do not believe. Never spoken in dialogue; the whole play proves it.
4. One play, one premise; two pull in two directions. Sub-premises may support the main one.
5. Not a universal truth, but inside your work it is the truth. No part outranks the whole.
6. Start anywhere, but find the premise before writing; a changed idea = a new premise. Sharpen a vague premise into a question and its answer; that dictates characters and the ladder of struggle.
7. The premise forces a single path: at each plot fork only one choice keeps it intact.
8. The obligatory scene proves the premise and carries peak expectation; a dull one means a blurred premise.
9. Dialectic: thesis (external demand) / antithesis (desire) / synthesis (ending). Prove why THIS person by excluding every other.
10. The ending comes from the character's actions, never accident or natural death.

## 2. Controlling idea = value + cause (McKee)
1. Premise = "what if...". Controlling idea = one sentence: the value at the climax plus its chief cause; a creative commandment.
2. Storytelling is the creative proof of a truth: idea turned into action, never explanatory speech.
3. Write the climax first, then ask: what value, what cause? The story tells you its meaning; a plot that ran exactly to plan was too tight.
4. Idea vs counter-idea: sequences alternate positive and negative charge, equal firepower; a foolish antagonist cheapens victory; no preaching.
5. Three kinds: idealistic (up), pessimistic (down), ironic (redemption / punitive plot). Irony is hardest: one action carries both charges, motivated and unambiguous, never cancelling.
6. One clear idea yields more meaning than many forced in. Great writers own one lifelong theme.
7. Do I believe the story's meaning? The artist's only duty is the truth.

## 3. The third rail: desire vs misbelief (Cron)
1. Story is not plot: it is how events change someone pursuing a hard goal, inside. Many events, no story = rejection.
2. Third rail = long-held desire vs the misbelief blocking it; every action, turn and sensory detail must touch it.
3. Five fallacies: prose first; free-writing; "all first drafts are bad"; outlining external events first; structure templates (structure follows story).
4. Spark to what-if, four steps: the moment that pierced you; why you care; the point; the what-if with context, surprise, conflict, implication.
5. Why, three steps: what he wants before page one (visible with eyes closed); why; the misbelief — not a "flaw" or "wound" but a belief held as true because once it WAS true.
6. Origin scene: where the misbelief took root, first person, in order: belief on entry / why / goal / how he expects to get it. Then three turning-point scenes: a step toward the desire, the belief pulls back; both strengthen.
7. The point grows from the inner conflict; the external what-if triggers it.
8. The ending is the moment the misbelief is beaten, not the last external event; write the epiphany early.
9. Primary external problem: external three (escalates to the last page, concrete consequences, ticking clock) + internal two (forces defiance of the misbelief, specific emotional cost).

## 4. Five premise questions, need vs goal (Hicks)
1. Premise = a brief statement of the main conflict. Five questions (5, as in the source): who is the protagonist? who is the opponent? why do they fight? what change does the outcome bring? why must the protagonist act? Then three sentences: one against another; why they must oppose; how his life changes.
2. Drama = conflict that visibly changes the protagonist's life and surroundings; choose events causally tied to that change.
3. Inner need: the quality he should have but lacks, thinks he has, or dismisses; he must gain it to resolve the conflict. Outer goal, two tests: the audience knows where it is and feels him threatened. Check plausibility against Maslow.
4. Theme is not a textbook; the audience wants shared experience, not lessons.

## 5. Theme fixed, attitude moves (Hoxter)
1. Three questions: what does he want? what does he struggle against? which theme (love, betrayal, loyalty, growing up, revenge...)? A romance is not necessarily about love.
2. Sweet spot: the theme never changes; the attitude toward it does. State it at start and end. Naive lead vs cynical friend, each with a lover = four interactions holding the script's values.
3. Theme test bench: run the same material through each candidate theme (growing-up, love, sacrifice, revenge, justice...); keep the one that makes the characters most alive.
4. Protagonist and antagonist both hold theme-related goals. Obstacle is not antagonist: only the antagonist expresses the theme.
5. Climax, three questions: does the villain's defeat express the theme? does the hero do what he could not in act one? does it close the plot line and the whole story?
6. Strong opinions on six things → four relationship characters take positions → thematic conflict plots itself.

## 6. One-line story (Snyder) and logline (Hoxter, McKee)
1. Snyder's four elements: ironic hook; vivid mental picture with a time frame; audience and cost; killer title. Enhanced: + hero/villain adjectives + primal goal. No sound one-liner = broken story; pitch a stranger and watch the eyes.
2. Ten genres, by what the story IS about: monster in the house, golden fleece, out of the bottle, dude with a problem, rites of passage, buddy love, whydunit, fool triumphant, institutionalized, superhero. Learn the rules before breaking them; never pitch "X meets Y".
3. Primal: survival, hunger, sex, protecting loved ones, fear of death; relationships as spouse, parent/child, ex.
4. Hoxter's logline checklist: protagonist, antagonist, genre, conflict, challenge, stakes, theme, twist, hook, inciting incident. Formula: inciting incident + protagonist + challenge + theme. Not a tagline. Plot = on-screen events; story = subtext and emotional journey; the logline leans to plot.
5. McKee: the title names character, setting, theme or genre; a literary, unspecific title is a fault.

## 7. Choose the subject strictly (Lu Jun)
Glosses: "choose the subject strictly", "dig deep", "fresh angle", "dramatic core".
1. Lu Xun's rule: be strict in what you pick and deep in how you dig; a trivial incident padded out is not a play.
2. Four taboos: chasing size (big subject is not big meaning); chasing fashion (one of a fad is worth a thousand); no dramatic quality (subject mismatched to form); wrong capacity (a full-length play crushed into one act).
3. Four "choose with": the heart (what struck you and stayed); the body (write what you know; tie period material to lived experience); the eye (artistic discovery, not others' glasses); drama (conflict, action, feeling; song for musical forms).
4. Six-character formula: small, simple, with a core. Event small; plot simple (tellable in five minutes; one spine; simple is not thin: complicate a simple matter); a killer move.
5. Three sentences: I have a good story; I have the passion to finish it; only I can tell it. The third matters most.

## 8. Dig deep = the governing idea (Lu Jun)
1. Originality shows in developing the subject fully (Goethe). Digging = the governing idea; think five moves ahead where others think three.
2. Four methods: big environment, small story (set it against its era); study a character (one real person with a trait); study the ending (digging IS studying the ending); the symbol.
3. Three "not equal to": not inflating; not preaching or illustrating (tendency flows from scenes and plot; a play equal to one slogan is an illustration); not feigned depth (if it stands without the good/bad labels, remove them).

## 9. Fresh angle (Lu Jun)
1. Subject collisions are harmless; works differ by angle. The same faithless-man frame plays from the abandoned side or from his own.
2. Paint the hidden temple as a monk carrying water: the cut that never shows the thing head-on and gets all of it.
3. Three techniques: ordinary into marvellous; compress time and space (one event, every inner life); condense emotion (not the men at sea but the family's fear).
4. Three angle demands for the short play: keyhole (one detail, not the panorama); focus (same event, different persons by character); deformation (a saint arrested; keep the effort on the social theme, never "dig wherever there is drama").

## 10. A good dramatic core (Lu Jun)
1. Tolstoy's focus: every work needs one centre of gravity, and it resists full statement in words.
2. Dramatic core = generative life in plot, explosive force in conflict, penetrating depth in meaning: the one thing that, removed, collapses the play (and makes it THIS play).
3. Core is the root; the "dramatic pivot" (a prop or key turn) is the branch; the "dramatic eye" (a key character or vivid scene) is the leaf. Test: remove it; if the play survives it was branch or leaf.
4. Four forms: a strange action; a chosen prop; a fate-situation; a song or a line.
5. Once the core is set, theme, structure and character follow.
6. Three sources: daily detail; the secrets of psychology; the imagined world.

## 11. Conflict must be true, plot must surprise (Lu Jun)
1. True conflict has three layers: real, meaningful, dramatic (not a debate). Eight ailments (8, as in the source): no social meaning; falseness; inaccuracy; no dramatic character (opinions clash, not temperaments); arbitrary misunderstanding; dodging the real fight; a feint (the threat never lands); no tension. Belinsky: both want to win, each stings the other's character, a new relationship results.
2. Plot must surprise but first be true: audiences want the tiger fight, not the dog fight. Surprise without social meaning is discarded. When the plot rings false, fix the character first.
3. Surprise from the plain is the basic skill; prefer flavour to ever more "correct" blandness.
4. Reshaping material, five Shakespearean moves: raise minor characters' minds; reposition the villain; sharpen the hero's wit, cut his passivity; plug plausibility holes through character; reset the ending so all converge for a fast close.

## 12. From idea to premise: working procedure
(12 steps, as in the source)
1. Catch the spark, write why you care (Cron); confirm only you can tell it (Lu Jun).
2. Four taboos and four "choose with" (section 7); event small, plot simple?
3. The point in one sentence → the what-if (section 3).
4. Set the premise (Egri: trait + leads to + outcome); confirm you believe it; one per play.
5. Third rail: want, why, misbelief; origin scene + three turning-point scenes.
6. Hicks' five and Hoxter's three; every main character's attitude to the theme.
7. Dig two moves further: three governing ideas, take the highest (section 8).
8. Find the dramatic core: remove it, is the play still there? Action, prop, situation or line?
9. Fix the angle: head-on or from the side? keyhole, focus or deformation?
10. Write the climax / epiphany; derive the controlling idea backward (value + cause); mark its kind; alternate idea and counter-idea (knowledge/screenwriting/03-story-structure.md).
11. One-line story (Snyder) and logline (Hoxter); say it to a stranger; record both in the bible (knowledge/screenwriting/01-workflow-and-story-bible.md; series: knowledge/screenwriting/09-series-engine-and-bible.md).
12. Self-check (below).

### Diagnostic checklist
(5 questions, as in the source)
1. Conflict clear of the eight ailments (section 11)?
2. Governing idea clear of the three "not equal to" (section 8)?
3. Are two premises pulling in two directions?
4. Is the ending caused by the character's actions, not by accident?
5. Same theme word throughout, attitude changed?
