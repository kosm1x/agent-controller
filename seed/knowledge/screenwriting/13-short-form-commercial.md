# Short-form commercial scripts: spots, explainers, testimonials, promos

**Read this when:** writing or fixing any 6–90 second piece for a brand, clinic, property, product or broadcast promo — TV/social spots, explainers, testimonial videos, listing tours, sales-force promos.
**Source:** authored 2026-09-12 for Jarvis (the screenwriting corpus has no short-form layer). Craft rules are mapped from knowledge/screenwriting/02-premise-theme-logline.md, knowledge/screenwriting/05-dialogue.md, knowledge/screenwriting/06-scene-craft.md and knowledge/screenwriting/07-format-fountain-adaptation.md; timing and mobile conventions are general industry practice.
**Pairs with:** knowledge/screenwriting/14-promo-piece-20s.md (the 20 s promo standard), knowledge/screenwriting/05-dialogue.md, knowledge/screenwriting/06-scene-craft.md, knowledge/screenwriting/02-premise-theme-logline.md.

## 1. A spot is one scene

A commercial is a single scene with the tools of a feature scene, compressed:

1. **One value turn.** The piece opens on a negative charge (doubt, friction, fear, boredom, a cost) and closes on the positive (relief, confidence, gain). The product or offer is the *event* that turns the value. No turn = an announcement, not a spot.
2. **One promise.** Before writing copy, state the premise in one line: *[audience] who [pain] can [outcome] because [mechanism].* Everything that does not serve that line is cut. This is the logline discipline of 02 applied to 30 seconds.
3. **One ask.** Exactly one call to action (CTA), stated once in VO and once on screen, with a verb: *Book, Download, Call, Visit, Reply.*
4. **Proof is specific.** A number, a name, a mechanism, a visible demonstration. Adjectives ("premium", "trusted", "world-class") are not proof and are cut on sight.
5. **Enter late, leave early.** Start mid-problem, not with a greeting or a logo. End on the CTA, not on a recap.

## 2. Lengths and beat budgets

VO in conversational English runs about 2.1–2.5 words per second. Leave 1–2 s of silence for the CTA to land. The word budgets below are derived as (runtime − 2 s of silence) × 2.5 words/s at the top and about 2.1 words/s at the bottom; a dialogue-led piece runs 15–20% fewer words.

| Length | Use | Hook | Problem / setup | Turn (product enters) | Proof | CTA | VO words |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 s bumper | reminder, retargeting | 0–1 s | — | 1–3 s | — | 3–6 s | 8–10 |
| 15 s | social, pre-roll | 0–2 s | 2–5 s | 5–8 s | 8–12 s | 12–15 s | 27–32 |
| 20 s | generic spot (the sales-force promo in 14 uses ≈35 words by D-014) | 0–2 s | 2–6 s | 6–10 s | 10–16 s | 16–20 s | 38–45 |
| 30 s | TV, YouTube, landing hero | 0–3 s | 3–9 s | 9–14 s | 14–24 s | 24–30 s | 60–70 |
| 60 s | explainer, testimonial, brand story | 0–3 s | 3–15 s | 15–22 s | 22–50 s | 50–60 s | 122–145 |
| 90 s | full explainer / patient journey | 0–4 s | 4–20 s | 20–30 s | 30–75 s | 75–90 s | 185–220 |

Rules of thumb at 20 s and above: hook never longer than 10% of the piece; proof never shorter than 30%; CTA gets the last 4–6 s at 20–30 s and may run longer at 60 s and above. Below 20 s the hook may reach 15%; the 6 s bumper's 1 s hook is exempt and it has no proof beat.

## 3. Spot architectures

Pick one architecture per piece. Mixing two is the most common cause of a piece that "says everything and lands nothing".

| Architecture | Beat skeleton | Use when | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem → turn → proof → CTA** (story spot) | doubt stated by a person → product enters → one specific result → ask | the audience has a felt pain and a known objection | the problem is generic ("life is hard") or the proof is an adjective |
| **Demo** | claim → show it working → the number → ask | the product is visual and the mechanism is the sell | shown from the company's POV instead of the user's |
| **Testimonial / journey** | who I was → the doubt I had → what happened (dated, specific) → where I am now → ask | trust is the barrier (medical, financial, cross-border) | the person recites features; no doubt is voiced; no date or number |
| **Explainer (how it works)** | the question → 3 steps max → what you get at the end → ask | the process itself is the objection ("how would this even work?") | more than three steps; steps described from the inside |
| **Objection spot** | the objection in the audience's own words → the answer with evidence → ask | one known objection blocks conversion | the objection is softened before it is answered |
| **Listing / tour** | the buyer's want → three shots that prove it → the one fact that closes → ask | property, venue, destination | shot list is a room inventory; no buyer want stated |
| **Offer** | the deadline or scarcity → what you get → the price/terms → ask | a dated promotion | the offer is buried after brand talk |

Brand anthems (mood, music, no claim) are not written by this method; they are a directorial form and rarely belong in a performance brief.

## 4. The hook (first 1–3 seconds, mobile)

Assume sound-off, thumb hovering, 9:16, no brand recognition.

- Frame 1 carries a face, motion, or a striking object; never a logo, never a title card, never "Welcome to".
- The first line is a question, a contradiction, a number, or the objection itself. Examples of shape (not copy): *"Two years fighting insurance. Nineteen days from first call to surgery."* / *"The price gap is real. Here is the number."*
- The first on-screen text (super) states the hook in ≤ 6 words, because most viewers read before they hear.
- Do not answer the hook in the hook. The turn answers it.

## 5. Writing the VO

Apply knowledge/screenwriting/05-dialogue.md to a single voice:

1. **Second person, present tense.** "You" is the protagonist; the brand is the mentor, never the hero.
2. **Short words, active voice, one idea per sentence.** Cut every "we are proud to", "our team of", "solutions".
3. **No brand monologue.** Three consecutive sentences about the company is a monologue; convert at least one into the audience's want or doubt.
4. **Cover-the-names test.** Hide the brand name: could a competitor run this VO unchanged? If yes, the proof beat is missing a specific.
5. **Core word last.** In the hook and the CTA, put the word that matters at the end of the sentence.
6. **Read it aloud at speed.** If it exceeds the budget in §2, cut the setup first, never the proof.
7. **The CTA is a verb plus a next step** ("Book a video consultation", "Download the brief"), not a slogan.

Testimonials are the exception to "second person": the speaker is first person, and the craft is the *doubt voiced then answered with a dated, numbered fact*. Any claim, result or disclosure wording is the operator's review, not the script's.

## 6. On-screen text (supers and captions)

- One super per beat, ≤ 6 words, noun-led. Supers restate the VO's *proof*, not its adjectives.
- Captions (burned subtitles) carry the full VO for sound-off viewing; supers are separate and fewer.
- 9:16 safe area: keep the top ~14% and bottom ~20% clear of essential text (platform UI overlays); center text in the middle 4:5 zone.
- The CTA super stays on screen for the full CTA beat and is the last frame.
- Logo appears once, with the CTA, unless the brief asks for a lockup earlier.

## 7. Shots

- One shot per beat; 2–4 s per shot in a 20–30 s piece; a 60 s piece may hold a testimonial face for 8–10 s.
- Start every shot mid-action (enter late). No establishing shots, no drone reveal before the hook.
- The proof shot is literal: the number on screen, the document in hand, the room the buyer wants, the surgeon meeting the patient.
- Avoid the static locations of knowledge/screenwriting/06-scene-craft.md (restaurants, bars, cafes, cars, dinner tables, offices, phone calls) unless the product lives there.
- If the piece is generated (image-to-video, stock), write the shot as a prompt-able sentence: subject, action, setting, light, lens feel, 9:16.

## 8. Script format for spots

Default delivery is a beat table; use Fountain (07) only for dialogue-driven spots with two or more characters.

```
# | Time      | Shot                                  | VO                                      | Super / caption          | SFX / music
1 | 0:00–0:02 | CU patient at kitchen table, morning   | Two years fighting insurance.           | "2 years. Nowhere."      | room tone
2 | 0:02–0:06 | …                                     | …                                       | …                        | …
```

Columns are fixed. Time is cumulative. VO cells hold only spoken words. The last row is the CTA row and names the logo lockup.

## 9. Self-check before delivery

(12 questions)

1. Can the premise be said in one line, and does every beat serve it?
2. Is there one value turn, from a stated negative to a stated positive?
3. Does frame 1 carry a face, motion or object, and no logo?
4. Is the hook ≤ 10% of the runtime (≤ 15% below 20 s; the 6 s bumper's 1 s hook is exempt) and left unanswered until the turn?
5. Is the proof a number, name, mechanism or demonstration, and ≥ 30% of the runtime (≥ 25% at 15 s; no proof beat at 6 s)?
6. Does the VO fit the word budget when read aloud at speed?
7. Does the VO pass the cover-the-names test?
8. Is there exactly one CTA, with a verb, in VO and on screen, in the last beat?
9. Is every super ≤ 6 words, one per beat, inside the safe area?
10. Does each shot start mid-action, one shot per beat?
11. Are all adjectives that stand in for proof removed?
12. Is the architecture single, and named at the top of the script?

## 10. Anti-patterns

- Opening with the logo, the company name, or "Welcome to".
- "We" stacking: three sentences starting with the brand.
- Feature lists where a single proof would do.
- A testimonial with no doubt and no date.
- Two CTAs, or a CTA that is a slogan.
- Music-only pieces submitted against a performance brief.
- A 30 s script that is a cut-down 60 s: rebuild from the premise instead.
