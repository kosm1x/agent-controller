# Story structure: paradigm, beats, acts and turning points
**Read this when:** outlining a script, placing plot points and the midpoint, fixing a saggy second act, designing an opening or ending, diagnosing "nothing happens", choosing linear vs non-linear design, or turning a premise into beats and cards.

Hicks, Screenwriting 101; Lu Jun, Playwriting Theory and Technique.

## 1. The hierarchy of structure (McKee)
- Structure = selected events composed into a strategic sequence to arouse specific emotions and express a view of life.
- Event = change expressed through a value (life/death, love/hate — a binary that can flip sign).
- Levels: beat (action/reaction) → scene (conflict that turns a value on at least one level; minor change) → sequence (2–5 scenes rising to a peak; moderate change; title each) → act (capped by a major reversal) → story (the final climax is absolute and irreversible; lower-level changes can reverse).
- No scene without a turn: note the value's charge at start and end; if equal, it is exposition — cut it (knowledge/screenwriting/06-scene-craft.md).

### The story triangle
| Corner | Design | Traits |
|---|---|---|
| Top | Archplot (classical) | active protagonist, external antagonism, linear time, causal, closed ending; widest audience |
| Left | Miniplot | open ending, inner conflict, passive or multiple protagonists |
| Right | Antiplot | non-linear time, coincidence, inconsistent realities; a rule broken once becomes the rule |
| Below | Nonplot | static; no story |

## 2. Three-act paradigm and plot points (Field)
```
Act I  Setup            Act II  Confrontation          Act III  Resolution
pp. 1–30                pp. 30–90                      pp. 90–120
         Plot Point I (pp. 25–27)        Plot Point II (pp. 85–90)
```
- One page = one minute. A plot point is an incident woven into the story that spins it in a new direction; the two act-end ones are the anchors.
- Know four things before writing: ending, opening, Plot Point I, Plot Point II. Ending first.
- First ten pages: who the main character is, the dramatic premise (what it is about), the dramatic situation (circumstances). Setup done by p. 30.
- Sequence = scenes bound by one idea — the spine; fix the idea before the content.

## 3. Save the Cat beat sheet (Snyder, 110 pages)
| # | Beat | Page | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening Image | 1 | tone, genre, scale; opposite of the final image |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5 | said to the hero by someone else |
| 3 | Set-Up | 1–10 | A-story cast introduced; six things that need fixing |
| 4 | Catalyst | 12 | bad news that is really good |
| 5 | Debate | 12–25 | last chance to refuse; pose a question |
| 6 | Break into Two | 25 | never later; the hero chooses to go, not tricked |
| 7 | B Story | 30 | usually the love story; carries the theme |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 30–55 | the promise of the premise; trailer material |
| 9 | Midpoint | 55 | false victory or defeat; mirror of All Is Lost |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 55–75 | villains regroup outside, team splits inside |
| 11 | All Is Lost | 75 | worse than the start; whiff of death |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75–85 | the lesson needs the defeat |
| 13 | Break into Three | 85 | answer from the B story / theme; A and B fuse |
| 14 | Finale | 85–110 | villains dispatched low to high; synthesis |
| 15 | Final Image | 110 | opposite of the opening |

**Scaling rule:** pages assume 110; scale each as a percentage of your length (catalyst ≈11%, break into two ≈23%, midpoint 50%, All Is Lost ≈68%, break into three ≈77%). The 77% collides with McKee's "last act ≤20 min" (§7); both are feature rules — take the shorter third act (break at 78–80%). Percentages are proportions, not law.

### The board (40 cards)
- Four rows: 1–25 / 25–55 / 55–85 / 85–110; each row ends on its turning point.
- Pin the most exciting scenes → see the gaps → pin the three turns (midpoint, All Is Lost as its mirror, Break into Three).
- Failures: 20 cards in act one (backstory — compress to one); act three with only two — revisit the six things to fix and the B story.
- Each card carries **+/-** (the scene's emotional change; unnameable = discard) and **><** (who has the goal, who blocks, who wins; one conflict per scene).

## 4. Nine beats (Hoxter) and the A/B story
- Act I (<30 min): **Basic Exposition** (problem or desire; inciting event) → **Debate** (wants it, does not know how; a weak first commitment).
- Act II (≈45 min): **Initial Progress** → **Raising the Stakes** → **Midpoint** (second commitment, no way back; protagonist becomes hero) → **Commitment** (help arrives) → **Crisis** (lowest point plus revelation).
- Act III (<30 min): **Confrontation** (the plan meets counterattack) → **Resolution** (goal met, theme shown, world changed).
- **A story** = main line, surface want. **B story** = the key relationship, strongest in act two; the B character helps build the act-three plan, executing it resolves A. Theme never changes — only attitudes toward it.

## 5. Three acts as attraction / anticipation / satisfaction (Hicks)
- Act I creates **attraction** (the audience agrees to follow); Act II manages **anticipation** (they keep guessing and guessing wrong); Act III pays **satisfaction** (every expectation met, none the expected way).
- **Ten elements**: backstory by subtraction; no flashbacks; inner need; the trigger; external goal; preparation; a stronger antagonistic force; self-revelation; obsession; a fight the hero wins personally; problem solved.
- Every scene advances the external goal and exposes the inner need.
- Premise five: who? wants what? why now? who stops them? result? (knowledge/screenwriting/02-premise-theme-logline.md)

## 6. Chinese short-play structure (Lu Jun)
### Opening–development–turn–conclusion = "phoenix head, pig belly, leopard tail"
- **Opening**: first clash, fuse lit; both sides' forces shown; short, exposition folded into action.
- **Development**: about two thirds; link by link, step by step higher, fast then slow — spur the horse while holding the reins.
- **Turn**: the climax peak, character and theme deepest — thunderous.
- **Conclusion**: right after the turn; brief, with aftertaste.

### Rule 1 — Enter the story fast
- Near not far (first cut close to the climax); simple not busy (one small matter, three twists); active not static (open on a dramatic situation). Drama fears explanation.
- Eight openings: (1) conflict — the quarrel already on; (2) array — the conflict situation laid out; (3) colouring — the main event pre-coloured; (4) surprise — an unexpected arrival; (5) opposite direction — a character heads the wrong way; (6) head-on blow — a sudden demand on the hero; (7) self-introduction — chorus, host or phone call; (8) forced over the wall — caught mid-misdeed, no retreat.

### Rule 2 — A clear through-line
- Find the play's true heart as the criterion for keeping and cutting: full where character is revealed; brief elsewhere.
- Subtraction (cut people and events); addition (supply grounds for abnormal acts); multiplication (exaggerate one laughable element); division (wrong entry point → start over).

### Rule 3 — A high climax
- No writing until the climax is found. Sources: character-driven; environment-driven; accident.
- Four diseases: no climax (fragments of equal weight); too early; not high (loud but the plot skids); artificial (kneeling, divorce, a slap — unless character makes it inevitable).

### Rule 4 — Smooth transitions (untying the knot)
- Eight taboos: passing the conflict upward; light cure for a grave illness; off-topic; long sermons; offstage handling; calling reinforcements; a chance blood-tie discovery; the stale trap plot.
- Three forces: environment (hand the unchangeable villain to an irresistible social force); groundwork (a motif escalates — love song → protest song → password); action — best of all one masterstroke.

### A decisive ending
- Decide the ending first. Four taboos: too round (an ideal halo), too long, too jumpy (a sudden miracle), too soft (offstage lines to close).
- Eight endings: (1) thunderclap — climax and ending fused; (2) suspended — a question left to the audience; (3) extended — a stammer never finished; (4) rebound — a cold pivot away from the tragedy; (5) finishing touch — one speech lights the play; (6) echo — the opening's motif, light then heavy; (7) cycle — back to the opening moment; (8) conclusion — a draw; a summarising chorus.

### Rule 5 — Much variation
- Six kinds: relationship, character, fortune, conflict situation, atmosphere, narrative mode. Source priority: character > craft > accident > outside force. Five or six clear changes pass; fifteen is exemplary.

## 7. Inciting incident and progressive complications (McKee)
### Inciting incident
- Five-part design: inciting incident → progressive complications → crisis → climax → resolution.
- Dynamic and fully developed, upsetting balance to plus or minus; the hero must react (refusal counts); it arouses the desire to restore balance → object of desire → quest; a complex hero also gains an unconscious desire.
- On screen; poses the major dramatic question; projects the obligatory scene.
- Within the first quarter; a late one needs a subplot to carry. As soon as possible, but not before the audience knows enough to react fully.

### Progressive complications
- Ever-stronger antagonism through points of no return: each gap eliminates an action of equal force. A "soft belly" = repeating act-one-scale actions.
- The gap: minimal action → the world reacts harder → a riskier second action. Risk test: what is lost by failing? "Back to normal" = not worth telling.
- Complicated (one level) vs complex (inner / personal / extra-personal turning at once).
- Four progressions: social (outward); personal (inward); symbolic ascension (particular to universal, invisible); ironic ascension (too late; led by being pushed away; discards the indispensable; self-defeating pursuit; the blow rebounds; doom that is a gift).

### Act design
- At least three major reversals. Act one ≈25%; last act shortest, ≤20 min (§3). Act-two swamp cures: subplots (each with its own act structure) or more acts (cost: multiplied climaxes breed cliché). Three acts plus subplots is the standard.
- The last two act climaxes must not share a charge; false endings suit few films.
- Four subplot relations: contradict the controlling idea; resonate; set up a late main plot; complicate the main plot (most important). None → a split story. Control weight so the subplot does not hijack the genre.

## 8. Crisis, climax, resolution (McKee)
- **Crisis** = decision; all actions spent but one; the obligatory scene; a true dilemma (two goods, or the lesser evil); a deliberate static moment, never off-screen. Crisis and climax may sit apart but must be cut together — never cut away to a subplot. Inside the climax, pry the gap open once more.
- **Climax**: the supreme reversal needs no noise but must be full of meaning — meaning produces emotion; inevitable yet unexpected (Aristotle); what they want, not the way they expect (Goldman); emotional satisfaction, not a happy ending. Key image: one picture for the eye that carries the controlling idea.
- Once found, rewrite from the end backwards; cut what changes nothing. Subplots climax inside the main climax, or least important first.
- **Resolution**: a scene for subplots unresolved in the climax; the climax's reach; one slow shot to carry the audience out.
- Hoxter's test: villain's defeat expresses the theme? hero does what act one could not? whole story closed at once?
- Coincidence: early so meaning accrues; none after the midpoint; never turn the ending on one (deus ex machina; antiplot excepted).

## 9. Non-linear, multi-plot and ensemble
- Non-sequential designs — Hoxter: sequences still cohere; a frame can be circular.
- Multi-plot; multi-part; ensemble (one inciting event touching every line); minimal plot.
- McKee: multiple protagonists → multi-plot glued by resonance, not causality; cohesion = how the strands answer each other.
- Flashback: dramatise it and first create the need to know (McKee); Hicks — none.
- Series structure: knowledge/screenwriting/08-series-structure.md.

## 10. Structure diagnostic
(13 questions, as in the source)
1. Can you say in one or two sentences what it is about (who, where, doing what)?
2. Do you know the ending, opening, Plot Point I and II? Is the midpoint a false victory or defeat, mirrored by All Is Lost?
3. Do the first ten pages set hero, premise and situation? Does setup run past p. 25? Does the catalyst land after p. 20?
4. Does the hero choose to enter act two? Act, or only react? Ask questions (many question marks in dialogue = a problem)?
5. Does every scene's value change from start to end? Are the unchanged scenes exposition in disguise?
6. Does each act-two gap eliminate an action of equal force? Any soft belly repeating act-one scale? Does it accelerate after the midpoint?
7. At the inciting incident, does antagonism outweigh the hero (an underdog)? Is the villain bad enough?
8. A whiff of death near p. 75? Is the crisis a static dilemma? Is the climax inevitable yet unexpected? Is there a key image?
9. Which of contradict / resonate / set up / complicate does each subplot do? None = split story.
10. Is the last act the shortest? Do the last two act climaxes carry opposite charges? Does the resolution serve subplots and give breath?
11. Lu Jun's four: fast entry (near start, single focus, few puzzles)? high climax (not early, not skidding, not artificial)? smooth transition (not passed upward, not light-cured, not offstage, no reinforcements)? decisive ending (not round, long, jumpy or soft)?
12. Count the changes: at least five or six clear ones in a short play, generated by character first.
13. Was the right answer spoken and rejected in act one (the Chekhov/Lopakhin move), and is what was promised paid off — or deliberately withheld — at the end?

## 11. Workflow: from premise to script
1. One-line story (Snyder: ironic hook, a picture the mind completes, audience, title). Hoxter's logline: protagonist + inciting event + conflict + stakes + theme (knowledge/screenwriting/02-premise-theme-logline.md).
2. Fix genre and story-triangle position.
3. Fill Hicks' ten elements and premise five.
4. Ending first, then opening and the two plot points; find the climax's key image.
5. One-page beat sheet: theme p. 5, catalyst 12, act two by 25, midpoint 55, All Is Lost 75, act three 85.
6. Board of 40 cards with +/- and ><; act three holds more than two.
7. Step outline (McKee): one or two lines per scene; sketch a dozen versions and discard them; once the climax is found, rewrite from the end.
8. Tell the story aloud in ten minutes; never show the outline.
9. Treatment (60–90 pages, present tense, no dialogue, subtext written out); reorder scenes here.
10. Script: a flaw sends you back to the treatment; skipping the treatment is the slowest route.
11. First draft just gets it down → cool off, compress → polish.
12. Revise act two first. Snyder's nine: hero active; no talking the plot; villain badder; turns accelerate; emotional colour wheel; distinct voices; vivid supporting cast; later starting point; primal enough.
