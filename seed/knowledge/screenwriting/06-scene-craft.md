# Scene and sequence craft
**Read this when:** writing or fixing one scene, choosing a location, building a sequence, planting a prop or detail, designing suspense, or turning talk into action.
**Source:** distilled 2026-09-12 from jtydhr88/screenwriting-skills `sw-scene-craft` (method only); underlying books: Robert McKee (Story; Dialogue), Syd Field, Neill Hicks, Richard Walter, Wendy Henson, Julian Hoxter, Mei Feng, Lu Jun (playwriting textbook; its four chapter titles are glossed in the section headings).
**Pairs with:** knowledge/screenwriting/03-story-structure.md, knowledge/screenwriting/05-dialogue.md, knowledge/screenwriting/13-short-form-commercial.md, knowledge/screenwriting/12-terms.md.

## 1. What a scene is

- **McKee:** action through conflict in roughly continuous time and space that turns a value-charged situation on at least one level. No scene without a turn. Test: note the value's charge at top and end; if identical, the scene is exposition: cut it, weave the facts elsewhere. One action across four locations is still one scene.
- **Mei Feng's value test:** the one thing to settle is which value is on the table and whether it flips or gains a layer by the end. Change only through conflict; conflict only through character choice (Liberty Valance's restaurant scene: civilization vs. savagery flips repeatedly).
- **Field:** elements are place and time; changing either makes a new scene. Decide the **purpose** first (the one fact that moves the story), then place and time, then the **components inside**.
- **Hicks:** every scene must both give new plot information and reveal more of the protagonist. Nothing happens = cut.
- **Henson:** one complete dramatic action, one place, consistent time, purpose and cast; new scene on a change of any of these.
- **Lu Jun:** a scene is not two lines of slug; ask why here and now (Lao She's cook: in his kitchen he is inert; feeding troops mid-battle he is drama).

## 2. McKee's five-step scene design

1. **Define the conflict:** who drives, what they want (as a purpose clause); what the opposing force wants, opposing head-on, not tangentially.
2. **Note the opening value** and its charge.
3. **Break into beats.** A beat is one unit of action/reaction; label subtextual action/reaction with emotionally coloured gerund phrases (grovelling / ignoring the plea). New beat only when behaviour visibly changes.
4. **Note the closing value** and compare (same = non-event).
5. **Survey the beats** for arc and turning point: each builds on the last until the charge flips. A turn comes only from **action or revelation**; four effects: surprise, curiosity, insight, new direction.

Supplements:
- **Scene intention vs. super-intention:** what each character wants now (scene stops when met) and unconsciously (often the reverse of characterization).
- **Five steps of behaviour:** desire, awareness of antagonism, choice, action, expression. The scene lives in the action taken by speaking.
- **Earliest symptom of a bad scene:** repeated beats (same tactic, new words).
- **Four diseases:** desires that do not oppose; circular repetition; a turn too early or late; on-the-nose. Cure: test each beat against the objective.
- **McKee's Dialogue scene types** (balanced, comic, asymmetric, indirect, reflexive, minimal), each with its own pattern: knowledge/screenwriting/05-dialogue.md.
- **Hoxter:** a beat is a moment of dramatic progress or a shift of the centre of gravity. Each significant character has an **objective**; the actor picks **tactics** (flirt, threaten, coax, reason); look for polarity reversal. August's six questions: what must happen; what if cut; who must be present; where; most surprising thing; long or short.

## 3. Entering, leaving, and pacing

- **Enter late, leave early.** Goldman: enter at the last possible moment (Chinatown: bathroom cuts to the after-scene in bed). Hicks: leave **before the conflict fully resolves**; unfinished business carries tension forward. Walter applies it to a single line too.
- **Rhythm (McKee):** 40–60 scenes averaging 2.5 minutes; a one-minute scene answers a four-minute one; two or three minutes drains a location (12 Angry Men: 80-plus French scenes). Shorten scenes toward an act climax to **earn the right to pause**; the big reversal scene is long and slow.
- **Tempo** = activity level in the scene.
- **Transition principle:** a third element two scenes share or oppose: trait, action, object, line, light, sound, idea.
- **Sequence.** Field: scenes tied by one idea (wedding, chase, homecoming), the spine of the script; fix the idea (cup) before content (coffee). McKee: 2–5 scenes rising to a peak; title each. Hoxter: scenes achieving story goals, unified by time or place; decide which beats carry weight.
- **Mei Feng:** between two heavy scenes place a short conflict-free one to slow the pulse.
- **Henson:** action paragraphs read like lists; carry the **given circumstances** (time, season, weather, physical clues). Sunny flags say Fourth of July; new snow says Veterans Day.

## 4. Action over talk (Walter, Henson, Hicks)

- **Walter, principle 30:** drama is behaviour. **Principle 31:** any action beats none; action fused with story is best.
- Film magnifies the small: the ring under the table (Loves of a Blonde), the French toast (Kramer vs. Kramer).
- **Static situations to avoid unless indispensable:** restaurants, bars, cafes, cars, dinner tables, offices, phones. If forced, make the object the centre of action. **Exercise: list twelve alternative locations for every scene**; keep the one that expands the plot and gives actors something to do.

| Original | Alternative | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Captain confronts boss in an office | Pool: boss swims laps, captain shouts from the edge, chlorine hits his 600-dollar shoes | Rank vs. patience; cop vs. money |
| Couple meets at a water cooler | Parking lot, changing a tire | Conflict through jack, hubcap, spare |
| Phone exposition | Booth used to find a package; phone thrown in snow | Phone becomes the action |

- **Hicks:** action passages have cause and effect and a **feel of rhythm**, never a shot list. Sex, fights, chases are hardest and stalest.
- **Henson's story-doing:** turn states into actions ("I'm sad" becomes weeping). A stalled script = characters stuck in inner states; use the "I want..." exercise. Write reactions against expectation.
- **Counterpoint (Field):** anger with a smile; talk flowers while falling in love. Comedy = a situation taken with utter seriousness.
- **McKee:** the best dialogue advice is not to write it; ask first how to do it purely visually.

## 5. "Interest must be full": suspense, delay, wit (Lu Jun)

- **Interest** = novelty, surprise, delight, craft, realised as **suspense** and **wit**.
- **Set and destroy suspense:** resolve one while planting the next. A huge opening question needs an ending that outweighs it.
- **Reveal vs. keep the secret:** Chinese opera usually hands the audience the key at once (Fifteen Strings of Cash names the villain early). Archer: no playwright can keep a secret from the audience. Curiosity is a one-night pleasure; the lasting pleasure is **foreknowledge**. The Yang Women Generals' birthday hall: hide the death (surprise) or let the audience know and watch forced smiles (far stronger).
- **Suspense of character and fate:** a poisoning revealed at the start still holds.
- **Delay to release:** crouch before the jump. A Night at an Inn shows the idol only at peak terror, then delays again; Mr Leonida stacks three layers between panic and false alarm.
- **Four sources of wit:** situation (Moliere's formula: conflict declared, the wronged plots, the target performs, the lashing, the exposure); character (a trait fired three times); language; detail.
- **McKee:** interest = curiosity + concern; mystery, suspense, dramatic irony; no cheap surprise.

## 6. "Details must be exquisite" (Lu Jun)

- The smallest unit describing person, event or setting. Story may be invented; details may not be faked.
- **Writer A vs. B:** A says the man "shows off wealth"; B shows him picking his teeth with a matchstick, a banknote hung on his belt. A states the general; B draws the individual.
- **Five functions** (from the comedy Beating the Gong): set the scene; drive conflict; draw character; build the climax (kill the duck to check its crop); point the theme.
- **Five requirements:** true; **close to the skin** (fits this play, character, scene); weighted (prefer details bearing their era's birthmark); **no showing off** (once is enough, seven times nauseates; explaining a detail's origin kills it; a repeat must advance plot, conflict or character); **visible** (saying the duck ate the grain is weaker than cutting it open).
- One source: observe life the way a waiter reads how a guest holds chopsticks.
- **Walter's "Conk":** not a small Midwestern town but Springfield, Ohio. McKee: concrete nouns, active verbs, no is/are.

## 7. "Props must be precise" (Lu Jun)

A prop is a small object that carries feeling; one well-chosen prop outdoes pages of narration; few and precise.

| Function | Example |
|---|---|
| Structural core | A locked box whose key is thrown on the floor, not handed over; the IOU in A Doll's House |
| Conflict driver | An object that moves the quarrel a step each time it changes hands; Othello's handkerchief |
| Characterization | A patriarch setting clocks by his watch; the three sticks in The Orphan of Zhao |
| Symbol of mind and fate | The Magic Chair (who sits, what they say before and after) |
| Window on theme | Cradle; the coffin in Peking Man |
| Song-and-dance vehicle | The millstone; the pot |

| Method | Example |
|---|---|
| Enlarge | Foot-warmer the size of a cauldron |
| Shrink | A whip for a horse; signboard like a playing card |
| Repeat | A paper ring of stamped letters |
| Transform | Jumping into the lotus jar and walking it |
| Virtual | Snow Mother; the Bodhisattva |
| Stand-in / live prop | An actor playing the object |

Accuracy is not poetry. Indick: the hero's weapon is an identity symbol; use it at the climax. Campbell: motifs recur, not overused. **McKee's image system:** one image category recurring start to finish, consistent yet varied, kept subliminal (blindness/water in Chinatown); once noticed it turns to neutral curiosity. Snyder: the callback; a repeated action reversed late.

## 8. "The setting must fit" (Lu Jun)

- **O'Neill's deliberate arrangement:** outdoor (horizon as desire) alternating with indoor acts; the drum in The Emperor Jones rising from pulse to frenzy.
- **Chekhov's wholeness:** rattle, axes, snapped string, pauses, all drawing states of mind.
- **Opera's contrast:** birthday hall turned mourning hall.
- **Avoid:** every rural play in a courtyard, every urban one in a living room. **Three criteria:** serves the theme (a tunnel niche for two); serves character (a mud pit carrying three layers of history); serves conflict (the elevator joins the fight).
- **Hardware and software:** the physical place vs. the literary layer (recurring song, folk tale, history).
- **Hicks by genre:** relationship drama with too much space loses forced proximity; comedy's world is a banana peel; horror twists corridors into mazes; detective stories rot in the city. McKee: a small, knowable world.
- **Egri:** entrances and exits only when the plot needs them; sending someone to fetch water is unforgivable. Lu Jun: bring the principal on as the storm breaks.

## 9. Scene diagnostic
(13 questions, as in the source)

1. Why here and now? Would another place make the conflict harder, the character more exposed? Listed twelve alternatives?
2. What value is at stake, charged how at top and end? Unchanged = exposition: cut.
3. What does each person want, by what tactic, and unconsciously? Head-on or grazing opposition?
4. Beats in gerunds: any repeats? Each closer to the turn? Turn by action or revelation, neither early nor late?
5. Entered late enough, left early enough, before the conflict resolves?
6. Any stretch where nothing happens? Characters stuck in states instead of actions?
7. Could it play without dialogue? Which small gesture can be magnified?
8. Are phone, car, restaurant, office unavoidable, and if used, the centre of action?
9. Suspense as one-night pleasure or foreknowledge of fate? Key handed to the audience? Delay layered (three build-ups)?
10. Details true, close to the skin, era-stamped, visible? Any showing off?
11. Which of the six prop functions, and which of the six methods?
12. Does the setting have software (song, story, history)? Entrances and exits required by the plot?
13. Sequence has one idea? Third element bridging adjacent scenes? Scenes shortening toward the act climax?

## 10. Workflow: writing a scene

1. In the step outline confirm the **purpose** (one advancing fact plus one protagonist revelation) and place in the act. Use the step-outline line format from knowledge/screenwriting/01-workflow-and-story-bible.md (scene number | int/ext, place, day/night | one-line purpose | value open -> close | structural position).
2. Write the **value pair** and opening charge; each character's intention, tactic, super-intention.
3. **Place:** list twelve, pick by the three criteria; decide the software.
4. Fix **prop and details:** the core prop's job; two or three visible close-to-the-skin details; whether any repeat advances anything.
5. **Suspense strategy:** audience knows more or less than the characters? Which key handed over? Layers of delay?
6. Write the **beat sheet** (gerund action/reaction plus value shift), find the turn, confirm no repeats; visual first.
7. **Entry and exit:** late in, early out; closing blow on the last word or action.
8. Draft: present tense, concrete nouns, active verbs, no camera terms or "we see"; dialogue per knowledge/screenwriting/05-dialogue.md.
9. **Self-check:** the diagnostic; Walter's "$?" (worth the ticket?) and "3 Strikes" (purpose? value? best way?).
